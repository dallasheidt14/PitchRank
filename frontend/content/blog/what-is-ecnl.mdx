ECNL stands for the **Elite Clubs National League**. It's a youth soccer platform [sanctioned by US Club Soccer](https://usclubsoccer.org/ecnl/), which describes it as "a top, league-based platform for both girls and boys players." It runs boys and girls competitions across the United States, organized into regional conferences with a national postseason.

The acronym is the easy part. What confuses most parents is that "ECNL" is used loosely to mean three different things — so this page separates them.

## The Three Tiers, and Why They Get Confused

The ECNL isn't one competition. It's a pyramid of three, and people use the parent name for all of them.

| Tier                     | What it is                          | Who plays in it                                                                |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **ECNL**                 | The top platform                    | Boys: 170+ clubs across 15 conferences. Girls: 129 clubs across 10 conferences |
| **ECNL Regional League** | The development tier directly below | Clubs earning their way toward the top platform                                |
| **Pre-ECNL**             | The entry tier for younger players  | Younger age groups, feeding the pyramid from below                             |

When someone says "my daughter plays ECNL," they might mean any of the three. When a club advertises itself as "an ECNL club," it may hold a place in the top platform in some age groups and the Regional League in others — clubs are admitted per age group, not as a single blanket membership.

## What Is the Difference Between ECNL and ECNL RL?

The Regional League is a real tier of the same pyramid, not a consolation label. The practical differences are competition footprint and what happens next.

The top platform is national in scope, with a broad postseason: the ECNL Playoffs draw roughly 300 qualifying teams across U13 through U18/19, alongside Champions League and Super Cup competition on the boys side and the League Cup on the girls side.

The Regional League is the tier clubs are promoted **from**. That promotion isn't theoretical — it happens in volume every year. For 2026-27 the ECNL announced 7 clubs promoted from Regional League Boys into ECNL Boys and 4 promoted from Regional League Girls into ECNL Girls, with 22 and 24 new clubs entering the two Regional Leagues respectively.

So the difference is real but permeable. Tier boundaries describe where a club was placed, not a clean break in team strength — the range of teams inside a single tier is wide.

## How Do Clubs Move Up?

By results, over time. The ECNL states that its "core value of competition rewards clubs for their performances on the field through multiple seasons and age groups, as they earn promotion into the ECNL or the ECNL Regional League."

Two details in that sentence matter for families:

- **"Multiple seasons"** — one strong year doesn't promote a club. The evaluation window is longer than a single season.
- **"And age groups"** — promotion is assessed across a club's age groups, not off one standout team.

In total the ECNL announced more than 60 clubs moving up or entering the pyramid for 2026-27, including 11 new Pre-ECNL leagues launching and age-group expansions in several existing leagues. The pathway moves a meaningful number of clubs every year.

## Is ECNL the Highest Level of Soccer?

For girls, it is one of the two top national platforms, alongside Girls Academy. For boys, it is one of two as well — the other being MLS NEXT, which is operated by Major League Soccer rather than sanctioned by US Club Soccer.

Our own ratings show how differently those two ecosystems sit. Among the top 50 U15 teams we rate, roughly seven in ten of the girls teams play in the ECNL, while on the boys side about three in five play in MLS NEXT. Neither figure says one platform develops players better — they show that the strongest teams in each gender are currently concentrated in different places.

For a structural comparison of the two boys platforms, see [how ECNL and MLS NEXT differ](/blog/ecnl-vs-mls-next).

There's also a "highest level" question the league table can't answer. Being rostered on a top-platform team isn't the same as playing meaningful minutes on one. Level describes the competition around a player, not the player's development inside it.

## What Pre-ECNL Is For

Pre-ECNL is the youngest tier, aimed at younger players and early development rather than national competition. It functions as the on-ramp: clubs that run strong Pre-ECNL programs build the results record that later supports promotion into the Regional League.

For families with younger children, this is the tier most likely to be relevant — and the one where the platform name matters least. At those ages, coaching quality and playing time dominate outcomes far more than which banner the league flies.

## A Short History

The ECNL launched in 2009 as a girls league. Boys competition was added in 2017, starting with what US Club Soccer describes as "57 of the top boys clubs in the country."

That ordering explains a common asymmetry. The girls side is the older and more established of the two, while the boys side has grown faster in club count since 2017 — now more than 170 clubs against 129 on the girls side. Parents who assume the ECNL is primarily a girls league are working from its first decade rather than its current shape.

## How the Season Is Structured

Teams play a regionalized regular season within their conference, which keeps most travel inside a geographic area. National competition happens at the postseason and at showcase events rather than through cross-country regular-season play.

The postseason includes the ECNL Playoffs, Champions League and Super Cup on the boys side, League Cup on the girls side, Conference Cup competition, and the Selection Game Series.

That structure is deliberate: regional regular season for cost and calendar sanity, national events for competition and college exposure.

## How to Tell Which Tier a Team Is Actually In

Because clubs are admitted per age group and market themselves with the parent name, working out where a specific team sits takes a little care. Four things that resolve it quickly:

1. **Check the schedule, not the club page.** A team's opponents tell you its tier faster than any marketing copy. Regional League teams play a Regional League fixture list.
2. **Ask which age groups the club holds, and where.** "Are we in the top platform at U15, or the Regional League at U15?" is a fair, specific question with a specific answer.
3. **Look at the postseason it is eligible for.** Playoff eligibility differs by tier, so the events a team can actually qualify for identify it.
4. **Remember it can change year to year.** Promotion happens annually and is assessed over multiple seasons, so last year's tier isn't automatically this year's.

None of this is hidden information — it is just spread across places parents don't think to look, and clubs rarely volunteer the distinction unprompted.

## Where the Money and Time Actually Go

Platform membership drives two costs that families consistently underestimate, and neither is a soccer decision.

The first is travel. A regionalized regular season keeps most games within a geographic area, but the national postseason and showcase events don't — those are the trips that require flights, hotels and time off work. Ask how many such events a specific age group travels to, because the answer varies by team, not just by club.

The second is the calendar. National platforms run long seasons with limited gaps, which interacts with school sports, other activities and siblings. A team one tier down with a lighter travel load is sometimes the better fit for a family even when the child would be competitive at the higher tier.

Neither point argues against the ECNL. They argue for asking about a specific team's actual schedule before signing, rather than inferring it from the platform name.

## What the Label Does Not Tell You

The most useful thing to understand about ECNL membership is what it describes: a **club's** standing, in a **specific age group**, based on **past results**.

It doesn't tell you:

- How strong your child's specific team is this season
- How much your child will play
- Whether the coaching in that age group is any good
- Whether the travel and cost fit your family

Those are team-level and family-level questions, and the platform name answers none of them. This is exactly why we rate individual teams across every league on one scale — a club's tier and a team's actual strength are different facts, and they disagree more often than most parents expect.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who sanctions the ECNL?

US Club Soccer, which describes the ECNL as a top, league-based platform for both girls and boys players. US Club Soccer is one of the two main sanctioning bodies for American youth club soccer, alongside US Youth Soccer.

### When was the ECNL founded?

It launched in 2009 as a girls competition. Boys competition followed in 2017, beginning with 57 clubs, and the boys side has since grown to be the larger of the two by club count.

### How many clubs are in the ECNL?

More than 170 clubs across 15 conferences on the boys side, and 129 clubs across 10 conferences on the girls side. Those counts change annually as clubs are promoted into and out of the platform.

### What is Pre-ECNL?

The entry tier of the pyramid, aimed at younger players below Regional League age groups. For 2026-27 the ECNL announced 11 new Pre-ECNL leagues launching, plus age-group expansions in several leagues that already run.

### How does a club earn promotion into the ECNL?

Through on-field results assessed across multiple seasons and multiple age groups, not off a single strong season. Clubs typically move from Pre-ECNL to the Regional League to the top platform, though some join from other leagues instead.

### Does a club join the ECNL for all its teams at once?

No. Placement is by age group, which is why one club can field a top-platform team in one age group and a Regional League team in another. This is a frequent source of confusion when comparing two clubs that both call themselves ECNL clubs.
